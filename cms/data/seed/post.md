## Why "resilience" changes the restructuring maths

Most cost programmes optimise for a single number: run-rate savings by a target date. Resilient restructuring optimises for two numbers at once — savings **and** the firm's ability to respond when demand returns. The second number is invisible on a spreadsheet, which is exactly why it gets destroyed.

The firms that recover fastest after a downturn are rarely the ones that cut deepest. They are the ones that cut **precisely**: removing structural cost while protecting the narrow set of roles and systems that carry institutional knowledge and customer relationships.

## Step 1 — Diagnose: separate structural cost from cyclical cost

Before any target is set, split the cost base into two buckets:

- **Structural cost** is embedded in how the firm is organised: layers of management, duplicated functions across business units, real estate, long-tail software licences. It does not come back when volume recovers.
- **Cyclical cost** scales with activity: contingent labour, overtime, marketing spend, travel. It self-corrects.

Setting a blanket "reduce headcount 15%" target across both buckets is the classic error. It takes cyclical cost that would have fallen anyway and counts it as a structural win, while under-cutting the genuine structural fat.

## Step 2 — Stabilise: protect three things

Draw a ring around these before the redesign starts:

1. **The demand-facing edge.** Sales engineers, key-account managers, delivery leads. These roles are expensive and easy to cut, and each one takes 9–12 months to rebuild to full productivity.
2. **Data integrity.** The people who keep the ERP, the CRM and the financial close trustworthy. Lose them and every downstream decision degrades.
3. **Institutional memory.** The managers who know why the last three process exceptions exist. Their knowledge is not written down anywhere.

## Step 3 — Redesign: build the target operating model

Design the structure you want in 18 months, not the one that is one cut away from today. Specify:

- The layers between the CEO and the front line (five is usually enough for a mid-market firm).
- Spans of control per layer.
- Which functions are shared services and which sit in the business units.
- The decision rights that move with the new structure.

## Step 4 — Hardwire: make it stick within a quarter

A new org chart reverts unless it is written into the systems that govern behaviour:

- Budgets rebuilt bottom-up against the new structure.
- Role descriptions and objectives rewritten for every changed role.
- The weekly operating review re-pointed at the new accountabilities.

If this is not done inside one quarter, the informal organisation quietly reconstructs the old one.

## Governance: a weekly cadence with one owner

Run the programme like an operational process, not a project. One accountable owner. A weekly forum that reviews savings booked, savings at risk, and capability risk flags. A single tracker that every workstream updates before the meeting, not during it.
